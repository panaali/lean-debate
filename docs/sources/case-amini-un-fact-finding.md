## Why didn't the U.N. publish its confidential evidence?

There are two main reasons.

First, **witness protection**.

The Mission explains that information is published only when the source gave informed consent and disclosure would not identify or endanger the source. Personally identifying information and confidential witness material were retained by OHCHR rather than included in the report because people inside Iran—and relatives of people outside Iran—could face retaliation. [Zagros Centre](https://www.zagros-centre.org/en/detailed-findings-of-the-independent-international-fact-finding-mission-on-the-islamic-republic-of-iran/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This wasn't hypothetical. The Mission reported substantial fear of reprisals among people considering speaking with it. [Zagros Centre](https://www.zagros-centre.org/en/detailed-findings-of-the-independent-international-fact-finding-mission-on-the-islamic-republic-of-iran/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Second, its mandate wasn't merely to write a public report. It was also to **collect and preserve evidence for potential future legal proceedings**.

It therefore stores evidence on controlled systems, attempts to preserve chain of custody, respects whatever limits sources placed on use of their material, and can provide appropriate evidence to qualifying judicial authorities subject to consent and protection requirements. [Zagros Centre](https://www.zagros-centre.org/en/detailed-findings-of-the-independent-international-fact-finding-mission-on-the-islamic-republic-of-iran/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Obviously almost none of those raw evidence items appear in the public report. [UNOG Newsroom+1](https://www.unognewsroom.org/teleprompter/en/2562/hrc-press-conference-independent-international-fact-finding-mission-on-the-islamic-republic-of-iran-14-march-2025/7693?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

# 1. Build a chronology before forming a conclusion

Create a precise timeline of:

1. the event itself;
2. when each piece of evidence was created;
3. when each piece of evidence became public;
4. when important claims were made;
5. when later evidence confirmed, contradicted, weakened, or clarified earlier claims.

Do not let later narratives contaminate interpretation of earlier evidence.

# 2. Create an evidence inventory

Identify all important evidence and classify it.

### Tier 1 — Direct physical or machine-generated evidence

Examples:

* original continuous video
* CCTV
* photographs
* audio recordings
* telemetry
* sensor logs
* flight recorders
* server logs
* GPS/location records
* laboratory values

* pathology
* autopsy findings
* ECG/rhythm strips
* medical charts
* original financial records
* source code / git history
* device metadata

* physical forensic evidence

* Is it original?
* Is it complete?
* Has it been edited?
* Is there missing time?
* Is provenance known?

### Tier 2 — Contemporaneous documentary evidence

Examples:

* contemporaneous medical notes
* police forms
* emails
* messages
* internal reports
* contemporaneous photographs
* official logs
* contemporaneous diaries
* emergency-service records
* contemporaneous legal filings

Prefer documents created before people had strong incentives to shape the later public narrative.

### Tier 3 — Independent expert analysis of primary evidence

Prefer experts who:

* inspected the actual primary material;
* explain their methodology;
* state limitations;
* identify uncertainty;
* do not infer beyond the data.

Give less weight to an expert who merely looked at a screenshot, heard a narrative, or appeared in media without seeing the underlying records.

### Tier 4 — Firsthand eyewitness testimony

Determine:

* Did the person personally observe the claimed event?
* Which portion did they personally see?
* What did they infer rather than observe?
* Did their story change?
* Was it given immediately or much later?
* Does objective evidence corroborate it?
* Could memory, stress, political incentives, group influence, or later information have changed recollection?

Never transform:

"I was there"

into:

"Everything this person says happened is therefore true."

### Tier 5 — Secondhand testimony

Examples:

* "someone inside told me..."
* family repeating what another person said
* journalists quoting unnamed intermediaries

Give substantially less weight.

### Tier 6 — Institutional conclusions

Examples:

* government investigations
* UN reports
* NGO reports
* corporate investigations
* parliamentary reports
* police investigations

Break their conclusions back into their underlying evidence.

Do NOT reason:

"The UN concluded X, therefore X happened."

Instead ask:

* What evidence did it possess?
* What evidence did it not possess?
* Which evidence was confidential?
* Which conclusions followed directly from physical evidence?
* Which depended on witnesses?
* What evidentiary standard did it use?
* Did it distinguish legal responsibility from medical/physical mechanism?
* Was the investigation independent?
* Were experts named?
* Could opposing experts inspect the same material?

Apply the exact same method to government reports.

Treat news primarily as a route to evidence, not evidence itself.

Distinguish carefully between:

"BBC reports that the father said X"

and

"X is true."

Prefer the original interview, document, video, or medical report whenever available.

Do not let a coherent story become evidence for itself.

A narrative such as:

"Police are known to use violence, therefore violence probably caused this death"

is insufficient.

Likewise:

"The government released medical records, therefore their explanation must be true"

is insufficient.

Patterns and priors matter, but event-specific evidence should dominate whenever strong evidence exists.

Use background patterns mainly as priors or corroboration, not substitutes for direct evidence.

# 7. Distinguish different causal propositions

Avoid collapsing related but distinct questions.

For example:

* Did physical violence occur?
* Was the violence severe?
* Did it cause a structural injury?
* Did it contribute indirectly through stress?
* Was it the primary cause of death?
* Was the terminal mechanism cardiac arrest?
* What caused the cardiac arrest?

These require separate probabilities.

Do not assume:

"Assault happened"

means

"Assault medically caused the death."

Likewise, proving a pre-existing medical condition does not prove that it caused the event.

For medical or technical questions:

* reconstruct physiological/technical mechanisms step by step;
* compare each proposed mechanism with observed timing;
* identify what objective findings should be present;
* identify missing decisive tests;
* distinguish pre-existing disease from acute cause;
* distinguish consequences of an event from its cause.

Example:

cardiac arrest → hypoxia → low EF

does NOT necessarily mean:

low EF existed before and caused cardiac arrest.

Likewise:

seizures after hypoxic brain injury

do NOT establish:

pre-existing epilepsy.

Use similar causal discipline in non-medical domains.

Distinguish:

* evidence known to exist but withheld;
* evidence that may never have existed;
* missing portions of a recording;
* incomplete medical imaging;
* confidential witness evidence;
* records destroyed or unavailable.

Do not automatically infer guilt from missing evidence.

But also do not pretend missing evidence has no effect.

State specifically how the missing information changes confidence.

"Outlet X is trustworthy."

* Was this particular claim sourced?
* Was the source primary?
* Was uncertainty communicated?
* Did the outlet distinguish allegation from fact?
* Did it update after contrary evidence?
* Did it apply equal skepticism to evidence favoring different sides?

If anonymous opposition witnesses are accepted easily while official documents require extraordinary proof, flag this.

| --- | --- |

| H3 | 15% |
| H4 | 10% |

## Probability assessment

Probabilities adding to ~100%.

Also give probabilities for important subquestions if useful.

## Why the probabilities moved

Identify the pieces of evidence with the largest effect.

## Source-quality assessment

Explain which sources handled the evidence well or poorly and why.

Do not rate them according to political alignment.

## Strongest counterargument to my conclusion

Present the best case against your own leading hypothesis.

## What could change my mind

List decisive missing evidence.

## Bottom line

Give the narrowest conclusion justified by the evidence.

# Mandatory reasoning rules

1. Never substitute reputation for evidence.
2. Never equate allegation with fact.
3. Never equate an official conclusion with underlying proof.
4. Never equate an international organization's conclusion with underlying proof.
5. Never infer a medical diagnosis from journalism alone when medical evidence exists.
6. Never infer absence of violence solely from calm behavior.
7. Never infer violence solely from later symptoms.
8. Never treat an anonymous witness as equivalent to video or physical evidence.
9. Never ignore a witness merely because they oppose your current hypothesis.
10. Never treat a family member as automatically reliable or unreliable.
11. Never assume a government is lying because governments have lied before.
12. Never assume a government is truthful because it produced official documents.
13. Never assume an opposition source is truthful because it challenges an authoritarian government.
14. Distinguish "possible," "plausible," "probable," and "established."
15. Explain when your own conclusion relies on inference rather than direct evidence.
16. Prefer "I cannot determine X from the available evidence" over filling gaps with narrative.
17. Search actively for evidence that would overturn your conclusion.
18. Revise your probabilities when evidence warrants it.
19. Do not force equal probabilities merely to appear neutral.
20. Do not confuse neutrality with truth-seeking. Follow the evidence even if the result favors one side.
21. Do not confuse confidence in an event with confidence in its mechanism.
22. Do not use people's certainty as evidence.
23. Treat retrospective memory as weaker than contemporaneous objective evidence.
24. Flag internal contradictions within the same source or side.
25. When two sources repeat the same underlying witness or document, do not count them as independent corroboration.

Your objective is not to produce a politically balanced answer.

Your objective is to produce the **best-calibrated reconstruction of reality that the available evidence permits**, while making the remaining uncertainty explicit.

This version is intentionally general enough to use for medical deaths, wars, political incidents, corporate disputes, accidents, scientific controversies, intelligence claims, or historical events.
