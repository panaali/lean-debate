Yes. What you are describing is very much studied in analytic philosophy, especially in **epistemology, philosophy of probability, philosophy of science, and philosophy of law**.

The basic idea is that rational reasoning does **not** require certainty. Instead, you ask: given the evidence I have, **how strongly should I believe this conclusion?**

Several approaches are common:

* **Bayesian reasoning:** update how plausible a hypothesis is when new evidence arrives. You do not always need an exact number like 73%; qualitative judgments such as “very likely” can still reflect Bayesian thinking.
* **Inference to the best explanation:** ask which hypothesis best explains the observed facts, compared with realistic alternatives.
* **Defeasible reasoning:** a conclusion can be rationally justified now while remaining open to revision if new evidence appears.
* **Base rates / prior plausibility:** extraordinary possibilities are not treated equally merely because they are logically possible. Your “elephant in the backyard” example is exactly this: unless there is evidence for an elephant, its very low prior plausibility gives it little weight.
* **Evidence strength:** distinguish direct observation, testimony, circumstantial evidence, absence of expected evidence, corroboration, conflicting evidence, etc.

Law does something similar but converts uncertainty into **decision thresholds**. For example, “preponderance of the evidence” roughly means _more likely than not_, whereas “beyond a reasonable doubt” demands much stronger confidence. Neither means absolute certainty.

So analytic reasoning is not restricted to binary:

**true / false**

It can instead reason:

**possible → plausible → likely → highly likely → effectively certain**, while explicitly keeping track of uncertainty.

A central principle is: **“logically possible” is not the same thing as “reasonable to believe.”**

**ChatGPT:**

Yes. The key point is that Einstein’s mistake was **not** noticing something silly; EPR identified a genuinely deep problem.

In the 1935 EPR argument, Einstein and collaborators essentially said:

> If quantum mechanics is complete, then measuring one particle seems to determine something about a distant particle instantly. Since that kind of nonlocal influence seemed unacceptable, quantum mechanics must be incomplete.

Einstein expected there to be some deeper, **local** description—roughly, hidden facts carried by the particles that quantum mechanics was failing to describe.

What changed everything was **Bell’s theorem** in 1964. Bell showed that no theory satisfying the relevant kind of **local realism** can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics. Later Bell-test experiments overwhelmingly agreed with quantum mechanics.

So, in hindsight, Einstein’s main mistaken assumption was:

**He believed nature should admit a local-realist explanation.**

He was essentially forced to choose among uncomfortable possibilities and chose the wrong one.

But an important nuance: quantum entanglement does **not** allow faster-than-light communication. The correlations are nonlocal in the Bell sense, but you cannot use them to send a controllable message instantaneously.

So Einstein was **right that quantum mechanics contained something extraordinarily strange**; he was wrong that this strangeness could be removed by completing the theory with ordinary local hidden variables.

**ChatGPT:**

Here is the simplest precise version:

* **Local hidden variables:** Each particle leaves the source carrying a hidden “instruction sheet” telling it what result to give for every possible measurement. Bell experiments rule this out.
* **Nonlocal hidden variables:** There is still a hidden underlying reality, but what happens to particle A can depend instantly on what measurement is performed on distant particle B. Bohmian mechanics is the main example.
* **Superdeterminism:** The particles’ hidden state and the experimenters’ later choice of what to measure were **never truly independent**. Both were already correlated by earlier causes. So when Alice “chooses” measurement X instead of Y, that choice was already correlated with the hidden variables of the particles. This avoids Bell’s conclusion by rejecting the assumption that measurement choices are independent of the particles.
* **Retrocausality:** Alice’s future choice of measurement can partly determine what the particle’s relevant state was in the past. So the correlation need not require an instantaneous influence across space; instead, causation can effectively run backward in time.
* **Many-worlds:** There is no single measurement outcome. Every possible outcome occurs in different branches. Alice and Bob become correlated through branching, without one particle sending something to the other.
* **Copenhagen-like interpretation:** Before measurement, some properties simply do **not have definite values**. Quantum mechanics gives probabilities, and measurement produces a definite observed result.

The especially strange thing about **superdeterminism** is that it says the apparent experimental freedom to choose “measure this way or that way” cannot be treated as statistically independent of the system being tested. That is why it can escape Bell’s theorem.

**ChatGPT:**

Not necessarily, but it can.

Superdeterminism specifically rejects **measurement independence**: the experimenter’s “choice” of what to measure is not statistically independent of the particles’ hidden state.

That does **not automatically settle the philosophical question of free will**.

* It conflicts strongly with **libertarian free will**: the idea that you could genuinely have chosen otherwise under exactly the same prior conditions.
* It can still be compatible with **compatibilist free will**: you act according to your own reasons and desires, even if those ultimately have prior causes.

So superdeterminism says less than “humans have no free will,” but it does make genuinely independent choices problematic.

**ChatGPT:**

In the Copenhagen view, there is usually **no detailed physical story about what “travels” between the particles**.

Before either measurement, the two particles are described by **one entangled quantum state**. They do not each have a fully definite individual value for the property being measured.

Then:

1. Alice measures her particle and gets, say, “up.”
2. The quantum state is updated: conditioned on Alice seeing “up,” quantum mechanics now predicts what Bob is likely to see.
3. Bob measures and obtains the correlated result.

The important point is that Copenhagen treats the **collapse/update of the wavefunction as part of the measurement description**, not necessarily as a physical signal flying from Alice to Bob.

And for measurements far enough apart, relativity may not even provide an absolute answer to **which measurement happened first**. So Copenhagen deliberately does not say, “Alice’s particle physically sent something to Bob’s particle.”

It essentially says: **the entangled pair is one quantum system; measurement produces definite outcomes with these joint probabilities.** It predicts the correlations extremely well, but does not give a deeper mechanical explanation of _how nature accomplishes them_.
