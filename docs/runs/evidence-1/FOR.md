# Evidence run 1: Agent FOR (assigned: K1 is TRUE)

Sources frozen at June 2026. All probabilities are subjective, evidence-weighted judgments.

**Narrowest conclusion.** Tribal Indians were, by the drafters' words in 1866 and by *Elk* (1884), held not "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" although some were prosecutable by US authorities; K1a–K1c are well supported. K1d, the step Krikorian needs, is weakened by the same sources: the ground was membership in a separate polity exercising its own jurisdiction, and *Wong Kim Ark* said so.

## 3. Exact disputed wording and plausible readings

**Amendment:** "All persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof, are citizens." **CRA 1866 §1:** "all persons born in the United States and not subject to any foreign power, excluding Indians not taxed."

**Howard, 2890:** "This will not, of course, include persons born in the United States who are foreigners, aliens, who belong to the families of embassadors or foreign ministers ... but will include every other class of persons." Reading A (appositive): diplomatic families only. Reading B (list): three classes. Howard never elsewhere treats resident aliens as excluded, and Conness (2891) said "children born here of Mongolian parents" would be citizens, uncontradicted. A fits better.

**Howard, 2890:** tribal Indians "are not, in the sense of this amendment, born subject to the jurisdiction ... They are regarded ... as being quasi foreign nations." **2895:** "a full and complete jurisdiction ... the same jurisdiction in extent and quality as applies to every citizen"; a tribal Indian "is subject for crimes ... to the tribe itself ... the jurisdiction of the nation intervenes and ousts what would otherwise be perhaps a right of jurisdiction of the United States."

**Trumbull, 2893:** "That means 'subject to the complete jurisdiction thereof.' ... Not owing allegiance to anybody else. That is what it means. Can you sue a Navajoe Indian in court? ... We make treaties with them, and therefore they are not subject to our jurisdiction." Later: "any Indian who owes allegiance, partial allegiance if you please, to some other Government" is not subject; "Does the Government ... pretend to take jurisdiction of murders ... committed by one Indian upon another? ... It is only those persons who come completely within our jurisdiction, who are subject to our laws, that we think of making citizens." Reading A: allegiance test. Reading B: exercised-law test. Trumbull's examples (suing, treaties, intra-tribal crimes) are all B; "allegiance" summarises B rather than adding a test.

**Johnson, 2893:** "We punish murder committed within the territorial limits in which the tribes are to be found." **Trumbull:** "Not except where it is done under special provision — not with the wild Indians of the plains." This exchange is the direct support for K1a and K1c together.

**Williams, 2897:** an ambassador's child who "commits the crime of murder ... to a certain extent ... is subject to the jurisdiction of the United States, but not in every respect; and so with these Indians."

**Elk, 112 U.S. at 101–102:** "not merely subject in some respect or degree ... but completely subject to their political jurisdiction, and owing them direct and immediate allegiance"; tribal-born Indians "are no more 'born in the United States and subject to the jurisdiction thereof' ... than the children of subjects of any foreign government born within the domain of that government, or the children born within the United States, of ambassadors."

**WKA, ¶63–64:** "*Elk v. Wilkins* concerned only members of the Indian tribes ... and had no tendency to deny citizenship to children born in the United States of foreign parents"; tribes stand "in a peculiar relation to the national government, unknown to the common law"; the Amendment "includes the children born within the territory ... of all other persons ... domiciled within the United States." **Fuller dissent, ¶192:** the words would be pointless "if they apply only to that obedience which all persons ... must pay the laws of the country in which they happen to be."

## 5. Strongest counterargument to my side

"K1 is true only in a sense that does no work. The drafters excluded tribal Indians because a second sovereign exercised jurisdiction over them inside US territory: Howard said tribal jurisdiction 'intervenes and ousts'; Trumbull said 'We do not exercise jurisdiction over them'; where the US did punish Indians it was by 'special provision' or under treaty clauses requiring the tribe to 'deliver up the wrong-doer', which is how one deals with a foreign nation. *Elk* adopted this: tribes are 'an alien though dependent power', and the comparison is to children born abroad. *Wong Kim Ark* then held that *Elk* 'had no tendency to deny citizenship to children born in the United States of foreign parents', that a resident alien 'is completely subject to the political jurisdiction of the country in which he resides', and that the tribal exception is 'peculiar' and 'unknown to the common law'. Undocumented parents belong to no sovereign exercising jurisdiction within the United States; nothing 'intervenes and ousts'. K1 refutes J17 only as loosely worded; the corrected premise — answerable to US law and not a member of a separate polity — survives, and K1d fails."

My only reply from the record is *WKA*'s "domiciled" qualifier and the silence of every inspected source on unlawful presence (no such federal category existed for the Chinese until 1882). That is an absence, not evidence.
